Does the method hold up?

This page is the honest version, not the pitch version. It covers what was tested, two real bugs that were found and fixed along the way, and exactly what the result does and doesn't support.

What was being tested

Khulna's rice fields go weeks at a time without a usable cloud-free satellite image during the monsoon. A reconstruction method tries to fill those gaps — but a method that looks convincing on a smooth chart isn't the same as one that's actually reliable. This study tested three reconstruction approaches against each other by hiding real, already-observed satellite data points and checking whether each method could recover the hidden values: plain linear interpolation, Whittaker smoothing (the approach used in this project), and STMS, a published 2025 method built specifically for this exact problem.

Three sites in the Khulna rice belt, each a single fixed point (not a blended area average), selected by scoring a grid of candidate points on seasonal signal strength and then manually checking true-color imagery to rule out villages and sandbars masquerading as farmland. 2022-2024, Sentinel-2 and Landsat 8/9, all free data.

Two bugs, found and fixed before trusting any result

1. Missing data was silently read as "clear sky"

The first pass used Sentinel-2's own cloud flag, which showed almost 100% "clear" skies even in peak monsoon months for 2022 and 2023 — not plausible. Checked against Earth Engine's own documentation: that cloud flag was discontinued for over two years and silently defaulted to "no cloud detected" while it was missing, rather than "unknown." Fixed by switching to a probability-based cloud mask plus explicit cloud-shadow detection, and verified the fix by confirming monsoon cloud dropout now shows up consistently across all three years, not just the one year the old method happened to get right.

2. One oversized test case was quietly skewing a result

Re-running the comparison under a looser cloud-detection setting appeared to reverse the entire finding — plain interpolation suddenly looked better. Investigating why found the real cause: one single test window happened to contain a quarter of one site's entire sample, and it was being weighted by how many data points it contained rather than counted as one test case among many. Fixed by weighting every independent test case equally. The original result came back — and came back more confirmed, not less, because the bug had been caught and corrected rather than quietly reported as a finding.

The result, checked three ways

The full comparison was re-run under three different cloud-detection strictness settings, not just the one that happened to look best. Lower numbers are better (they measure how far off the reconstructed values were from the real hidden ones).

What this doesn't claim

  • Not field-validated planting or harvest dates — the comparison is against other satellite observations, not ground visits.
  • Not a representative sample of "typical" Khulna rice fields — three locations were deliberately picked for strong seasonal signal.
  • Two of the nine monsoon-season site-years didn't have enough surrounding data to support a reliable growth-peak estimate even after adding a second satellite — rather than patch that gap, it's documented and dropped as a claim.
  • Not a peer-reviewed paper. It's a bounded independent study, built and checked solo.
Second study · October 2026

Flood detection, tested against 10 documented floods

Monsoon cloud hides the ground exactly when floods happen, so flood detection leans on Sentinel-1 radar, which sees through cloud. To find out whether it works, the pipeline was run against 10 dated floods and waterlogging events in Satkhira and Khulna from 2024 to 2026, each taken from press and official reports with its source (Cyclone Remal, the Betna embankment breach, the Bichhat and Dargahpur breaches, and urban waterlogging in Khulna City and Satkhira town). Each test was also run on the same dates a year earlier, to catch a method that just fires every monsoon. The detection rule was fixed before the first run and not adjusted afterwards.

Three bugs, found by running it on real data

Running the pipeline on real imagery for the first time exposed three problems, each fixed before any flood result was counted:

  • Cloud remnants read as floods. Optical scenes with almost everything masked out as cloud left a few edge pixels that read as 60–100% water. Scenes less than half clear are now skipped.
  • The radar threshold was too strict. The common published value (−16 dB) found a quarter to half of the water seen on clear days. Calibrated against 9 same-week clear optical scenes, −14 dB fits best, with moderate agreement.
  • Large areas were double-counted. One satellite pass covering several image tiles was being counted as several separate passes. Same-day images are now merged first.

The result: the original flood flag failed

Three ways of flagging a flood were compared. The original flag (40% of the area wet for 14 days) caught none of the 10 floods and fired just as often in years with no flood, wherever shrimp ponds already cover half the land. Comparing against water before the event, and against the same weeks in the five previous years, both caught 4 of 10. The seasonal comparison is the more trustworthy of the two: it catches the largest flood in the set with a quiet control year, and it rarely fires when there was no flood.

What it can see: large rural floods that leave water standing across a wide area, like the September 2024 Satkhira flood. What it can't, yet: embankment breaches that flood a few hundred hectares inside a much larger search area, and urban waterlogging, where shallow street water between buildings doesn't look like water to radar and often drains before the next satellite pass.

What the flood test doesn't claim

  • Not checked against mapped flood extents, only against reports that a flood happened somewhere in a broad search area.
  • 4 of 10 is not good enough for a payout trigger, and isn't presented as one. The plan is to report unusual water as a measurement, not a yes/no flag.
  • Ten events is a small test. Two of the control years turned out to contain floods of their own, which muddies those comparisons.
  • The radar threshold was calibrated on one small area in one year.
